        What the model includes

        • One star and one planet, attracting each other by Newtonian gravity, which weakens with the square of distance.
        • The star stays fixed at the centre. The planet's mass is treated as negligible, so it doesn't pull the star around.
        • A collision check. The star's radius is estimated as R☉ × M0.8, a rough main-sequence scaling used only to detect an impact.

        What it leaves out

        • Other planets, tides, drag and radiation pressure.
        • General relativity. Effects such as the extra 43 arcseconds per century that Mercury's orbit rotates won't appear.

        What's to scale

        Distances and orbit shapes are to scale; the rings mark distance from the star. The star and planet are enlarged so you can see them. At the default zoom, the real Sun would be about one pixel across. Arrow lengths grow with speed and with gravity's pull, but are compressed to stay readable.

        How it's calculated

        The trail is computed in small time steps with velocity Verlet, a method that keeps energy from drifting over long runs. In Auto mode, each step is short enough to take 1,500 steps around a circle at the orbit's closest-approach distance.

        The dashed line is not simulated. It is the exact conic section (circle, ellipse, parabola or hyperbola) that Newton's laws predict for the starting position and velocity.

        Model check thresholds: energy within 0.01% of the starting gravitational energy, angular momentum within 0.0001%, period within 0.1% of Kepler's third law, and wedge areas within 0.1% of each other.

        Why it's flat

        A two-body orbit never leaves the plane it starts in, because angular momentum is conserved. A 3D view would add camera controls without adding information. 3D earns its place once there are several bodies on tilted orbits.

        Constants and sources

        • Astronomical unit: 149,597,870,700 m exactly (IAU 2012 Resolution B2).
        • Solar GM: 1.3271244 × 1020 m³ s⁻², and solar radius 695,700 km (IAU 2015 Resolution B3 nominal values).
        • Year: 365.25 days (Julian year).
        • Comparison value: Earth's mean orbital speed is 29.78 km/s (NASA Earth Fact Sheet); the circular speed this model computes at 1 AU is 29.78 km/s.

        Review status

        Physics checked by automated tests on 15 October 2026: with default settings, a circular 1 AU orbit run for 100 orbits holds energy to better than one part in ten billion and matches Kepler's period to 6 parts per million. Not yet reviewed by an external astronomer.

            Equations used

            • δ = (Rp / R★)2depth, full transit
            • a3 = GMP2 / 4π2orbit size from period

            What the model includes

            • Exact overlap of two disks: the planet's silhouette against the star.
            • A circular orbit whose size follows from the period through Kepler's third law.
            • Random noise with a fixed seed, so the same settings always give the same measurements.

            What it leaves out

            • Limb darkening: real stars are dimmer at the edge, which rounds the bottom of the dip.
            • Starspots, flares, eccentric orbits and light from the planet itself.
            • Combining many transits, which is how real surveys beat noise.

              Sources and methods

              How this site decides what is true, and how far along it is.

              Which sources win

              Different claims need different evidence. When sources disagree, we prefer them in this order:

              1. Standards and official mission documentation, for constants, units, mission status and instruments.
              2. Original peer-reviewed research, for research findings.
              3. Curated archives and catalogues (NASA Exoplanet Archive, Gaia, JPL), for measurements.
              4. Review papers and university textbooks, for established concepts.
              5. Reputable educational material, for accessible explanations.

              A preprint is labelled as one and is not treated as peer-reviewed. An agency web article is not automatically the strongest evidence for a research claim.

              How the labs are validated

              Each lab runs automated physics tests. The orbit lab holds energy to better than one part in ten billion over 100 orbits and matches Kepler's period to 6 parts per million. The transit lab's depths and durations match the standard formulas to better than one part in a million. Each lab also shows live checks so you can watch them hold, or break.

              Review status
